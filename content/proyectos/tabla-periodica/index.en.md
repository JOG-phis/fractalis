## How to use it in class

- **Colour by** changes what the table shows: families, the s, p, d and f blocks, state at 25 °C, metals and non-metals, or **periodic trends** (electronegativity, atomic radius, ionisation energy, density and melting point). With the trends, students can see at a glance how they change down a group and across a period.
- Each element's **card** brings together its atomic mass, electron configuration, electrons per shell and the **common oxidation states used in nomenclature**. The A-level data (ionisation energy, electron affinity, radii…) are in a separate section.
- If an element appears in a unit or activity on this site, its card links to them.
- You can **link directly** to an element or a mode: for example, `…/tabla-periodica/?modo=en_pauling#F` opens the table coloured by electronegativity with the card for fluorine, and `?modo=tipo&ox=1` opens it with the oxidation states and metals and non-metals.
- The **Oxidation states** switch replaces each element's name and mass with its common oxidation states (negative ones in italics). The colours follow the chosen mode: with "Metals and non-metals", for example, you get an oxidation-state table ready for nomenclature.
- The **Print (A4)** button produces a clean landscape A4 table ready to photocopy, with the colours of the chosen mode (the family colours if you are viewing a trend) and, if the switch is on, the oxidation states.
- You can use it with the keyboard: tab into the table and use the arrow keys to move around.

## Where the data come from

The numerical data come from the open dataset [Periodic-Table-JSON](https://github.com/Bowserinator/Periodic-Table-JSON), based on Wikipedia (CC BY-SA). Element names follow IUPAC nomenclature. The oxidation states are the ones commonly used in secondary-school nomenclature, not every possible one. For the heaviest synthetic elements, several properties are theoretical predictions.
